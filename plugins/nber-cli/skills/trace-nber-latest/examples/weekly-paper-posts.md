# NBER Weekly Paper Posts

Abstract: This week NBER released 10 new working papers, covering monetary policy, family labor, gender attitudes, corporate innovation, and international sovereign debt. Among them, w35761, w35763, and w35765 are most relevant to labor economics and gender studies.

## w35761

### metadata
- title: Measuring Gender Attitudes Toward Men and Women
- authors: Joshua T. Dean, Christine L. Exley, Muriel Niederle, Heather Sarsons
- topics: ["gender", "gender attitudes", "survey methodology"]
- related-value: 0.xxx

### Why Read it?
If you care about gender issues, this paper introduces a paired role-reversed survey design that reveals how people judge the same behavior differently depending on whether a man or a woman does it. It turns out that many Americans still believe the social acceptability of an action depends on gender.

### Story
Traditional surveys ask "what do you think about women not doing housework" but never ask "what do you think about men not doing housework." That makes society look more progressive than it really is. The authors assign the same behavior to both genders and ask the same respondents, finding that only about half give consistent answers. This means many people do not actually hold gender-neutral attitudes; they simply apply different standards to different genders.

### Shine Point
Methodological innovation: the role-reversed design uncovers a hidden gender-neutrality gap that standard surveys miss. Sobering conclusion: American society is not as gender-neutral as headline numbers suggest.

## w35763

### metadata
- title: The Impact of Fertility on Mothers' Remote Work
- authors: Benjamin W. Cowan, Mohamed Hafis Zida
- topics: ["labor economics", "gender", "remote work"]
- related-value: 0.xxx

### Why Read it?
If you follow labor and gender, this paper uses ACS data from 2003 to 2024 to study how having children affects mothers' remote-work arrangements, and it finds a surprising reversal.

### Story
Before the pandemic, an additional child made mothers more likely to work remotely. After 2020, the effect flipped negative. Remote work did not disappear; rather, its nature changed. Pre-pandemic remote jobs were often high-income, flexible positions that suited mothers. Post-pandemic remote work became widespread and lost that selective advantage. In short, remote work in its current form has limited power to reduce the motherhood penalty.

### Shine Point
Long time span (2003-2024); clever identification (sex composition of first two children and twin births as instruments); direct policy implication: do not expect remote work alone to solve the child penalty.

## w35765

### metadata
- title: "The Evolving Marriage Gap in Children's Outcomes: Family Structure and Young Adult Outcomes Revisited"
- authors: Melissa Schettini Kearney, Phillip B. Levine, Alden Q. Barson
- topics: ["labor economics", "family economics", "inequality"]
- related-value: 0.xxx

### Why Read it?
If you care about family and labor economics, this paper revisits Kearney & Levine (2017) a decade later to see how the marriage gap in children's outcomes has evolved.

### Story
Using 2013 data, the original study found that family structure strongly predicted young adult poverty and college completion. With 2023 data, the poverty gap has largely shrunk, but the college-completion gap persists and is concentrated among children of more-educated mothers. Single-parent families have improved economically, but educational stratification remains.

### Shine Point
Heterogeneous framework plus a ten-year comparison; the marriage premium is diverging across outcomes: economic outcomes are becoming more equal, while educational outcomes remain unequal.
